March 15, 2024 Materials By Yuki Tanaka

The Beauty of Natural Materials

Why stone, wood, and clay continue to outperform their synthetic counterparts — not just aesthetically, but experientially.

There is a moment in every project when the material palette is presented to the client. The stone samples are laid out — honed limestone, brushed travertine, cleft slate. The wood boards are leaned against the wall — white oak, walnut, cedar. And inevitably, someone reaches out to touch.

This tactile impulse is not sentimentality. It is recognition. Natural materials carry information that synthetic ones cannot: the geological history in a vein of calcite, the seasonal growth rings in a timber board, the fire that transformed clay into ceramic. When we run our fingers across honed stone, we are reading a story millions of years in the making.

But the beauty of natural materials extends beyond the visual and tactile. It is performative. Limestone breathes, regulating humidity. Cedar ages to silver, protecting itself. Clay plaster absorbs and releases moisture, stabilizing indoor climate. These are not passive surfaces — they are active participants in the building's ecology.

Synthetic materials promise consistency. Natural materials deliver character. A limestone floor will never look the same in two projects — each quarry block carries its own fossil record, its own tonal dance. This variability is not a defect; it is the signature of authenticity.

In our work, we specify materials not for their novelty but for their honesty. We want the patina of use, the grace of aging, the deepening of beauty over time. A building that improves with age is a building that respects its occupants and its environment.

February 28, 2024 Light By Isabelle Moreau

Designing with Light and Shadow

Light is not merely illumination — it is a construction material. How we shape, filter, and choreograph it defines the emotional quality of space.

Louis Kahn famously said, "The sun never knew how great it was until it hit the side of a building." We would add: the building never knew its own form until the sun revealed it. Light does not just illuminate architecture — it completes it.

In our practice, light is treated as a primary design material, alongside stone, wood, and concrete. We model it, simulate it, and choreograph it with the same rigor we apply to structure. But unlike structure, light is temporal. It moves. It changes color. It appears and disappears. Designing for light means designing for time.

The most powerful architectural moments often occur in shadow. A deep window reveal catches morning light, casting a sharp geometric shadow across a stone floor. A perforated screen filters harsh midday sun into a constellation of dancing points. A clerestory brings north light into a gallery, rendering color with perfect fidelity. These are not accidents — they are composed.

We design for three light conditions: direct (sunlight), diffuse (skylight), and reflected (bounced light). Each has a different quality, a different emotional register. Direct light creates drama and hierarchy. Diffuse light creates calm and equity. Reflected light creates subtlety and depth. The art is in the balance.

At night, the choreography continues. We design artificial lighting not as a simulation of daylight but as its own composition — layers of accent, ambient, and task light that reveal the architecture's second personality. The building that is serene by day becomes poetic by night.

January 20, 2024 Nature By Thomas Andersson

The Relationship Between Architecture and Nature

The boundary between built and grown is not a line — it is a zone. Exploring the fertile threshold where architecture and landscape become indistinguishable.

The history of architecture can be read as a negotiation with nature. Sometimes adversarial — the wall that keeps weather out. Sometimes collaborative — the courtyard that brings sky in. Sometimes surrender — the ruin that the forest reclaims. The most compelling work lives in the collaborative zone.

We are interested in the threshold — the place where architecture stops being purely built and starts being grown. This is not about adding plants to buildings (though we do that). It is about designing the interface: the soil depth for a roof garden, the drainage for a courtyard tree, the structural capacity for a living wall, the microclimate for an indoor garden.

Biophilic design has become a buzzword, but its principles are timeless. Prospect and refuge. Complexity and order. Sensory variability. Thermal and airflow variability. Dynamic and diffuse light. Connection with natural systems. These are not trends — they are evolutionary imperatives. We feel better in spaces that echo the environments we evolved in.

In our projects, nature is not decoration. It is infrastructure. The olive trees in the courtyard provide shade, fruit, and habitat. The green roof insulates, manages stormwater, and creates habitat. The indoor garden filters air, regulates humidity, and connects occupants to seasonal change. Every green element earns its place through performance.

The ultimate goal is reciprocity. The architecture gives the landscape structure, irrigation, and protection. The landscape gives the architecture cooling, beauty, and meaning. Neither is complete without the other.
