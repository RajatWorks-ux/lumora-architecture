The Quiet House

A minimalist retreat of concrete, timber, and water — designed for stillness in the heart of historic Kyoto.

Typology Private Residence
Location Kyoto, Japan
Status Conceptual Design
Year 2024

Project Overview

On a narrow flagpole site in Kyoto's Higashiyama district, The Quiet House is an exploration of silence as a spatial quality. The residence turns inward, creating a world apart from the city's rhythm.

The plan is organized around a central zen garden — a carefully composed arrangement of stone, moss, and water visible from every room. A reflecting pool doubles the garden's presence, bringing sky into the subterranean level. Sliding shoji screens of washi paper and cedar allow spaces to expand, contract, or disappear entirely.

The material palette is reduced to three elements: board-formed concrete (walls, floors), Japanese cedar (screens, ceilings, joinery), and water. Each material is treated with reverence — concrete receives a hand-rubbed finish revealing aggregate, cedar is traditionally charred (shou sugi ban) for longevity, water is still and reflective.

The program is deliberately simple: a meditation room, a sleeping room, a bathing room, a tea room, and a study. No living room, no dining room, no kitchen — the client, a Zen practitioner, requested spaces for practice, rest, and contemplation only. Meals are taken in the tea room; the kitchen is a compact service core.

Design Concept

Ma — the space between

The Japanese concept of ma — the meaningful interval, the pause between notes, the space that gives form to form — is the organizing principle of The Quiet House.

The residence is not defined by its rooms but by the intervals between them. The zen garden is the central ma — the void that gives meaning to the surrounding spaces. Every room frames the garden differently: the meditation room offers a low, grounded view; the study, elevated, looks down onto the moss; the bathing room reflects the garden in the ofuro's water surface.

Shoji screens modulate ma dynamically. Fully closed, they create intimate, softly lit chambers. Partially open, they frame specific garden compositions. Fully retracted, they dissolve boundaries entirely, making the garden the room. The architecture breathes with the occupant's need for solitude or connection.

Acoustics are engineered for silence. Triple-glazed windows, floating floors, and absorbent ceiling treatments reduce external noise to below 20 dB. The reflecting pool's water feature — a single, slow overflow — masks residual sound with white noise. Inside, the only sounds are breath, footsteps on cedar, and the occasional temple bell from the hill.
