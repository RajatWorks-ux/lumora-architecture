The Cliff House

A dramatic residence cantilevered over the Pacific, where architecture surrenders to the raw power of the coastline.

Typology Private Residence
Location Big Sur, California
Status Conceptual Design
Year 2024

Project Overview

On a sheer basalt cliff dropping two hundred feet to the Pacific, The Cliff House does not dominate the landscape — it yields to it. The residence is a slender bridge of glass and concrete spanning between two bedrock anchors, its living spaces suspended above the churning ocean.

The plan is ruthlessly simple: a single linear volume, twelve meters wide and sixty meters long, containing the entire program. Public spaces — living, dining, kitchen — occupy the center, oriented toward the western horizon. Private spaces — bedrooms, baths — flank either end. A narrow service core runs the length of the landward side, housing circulation, storage, and mechanical systems.

The material palette is reduced to three elements: board-formed concrete (structure, floors, landward wall), structural glass (seaward facade, roof), and blackened steel (columns, beams, detailing). The concrete bears the imprint of Douglas fir formwork, its grain a tactile memory of the forest. The glass is low-iron, maximizing clarity — when the sliding walls disappear, the boundary between architecture and ocean dissolves entirely.

Key Features

  • 280 m² internal area
  • 120 m² terraces
  • 3 bedrooms, 3 bathrooms
  • 15-meter cantilever over ocean
  • Full-height sliding glass walls
  • Rooftop observation deck
  • Helipad & funicular access
  • Seismic base isolation

  • Blackened steel (columns, beams, connections)
  • Basalt stone (entry threshold, hearth)
  • White oak (millwork, interior doors)

Design Concept

Surrender to the site

The concept is one of minimal intervention. The site — a vertical cliff face, the Pacific horizon, the path of the sun, the migration of whales — dictates every decision. The architecture is a frame, not a picture.

The residence is anchored to bedrock at two points only, minimizing disturbance to the fragile cliff ecology. Between these anchors, the structure spans like a bridge, its depth calibrated to the cantilever: a tapered concrete spine, deepest at the landward anchor, thinnest at the tip. This structural logic is expressed honestly — the spine is visible in section, a thickened wall housing services and storage, thinning to a knife edge at the ocean-facing glass.

The roof is a plane of glass, bringing the sky into the house. By day it frames passing clouds and raptors; by night it becomes a skylight for stargazing. A motorized shading system, integrated into the glass roof, moderates solar gain without compromising the view.

Access is deliberately challenging: a funicular railway descends from the coastal highway to a discrete entry pavilion, or a helipad on the roof for those arriving by air. The journey is part of the arrival — the architecture reveals itself slowly, first as a slender line on the cliff, then as a transparent volume, finally as a sanctuary suspended above the void.
