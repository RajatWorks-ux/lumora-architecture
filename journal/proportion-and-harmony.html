Proportion and Harmony

Revisiting classical principles for contemporary architecture.

There is a moment in every project when the plan feels right. Not because it solves the program, not because it meets the budget, but because the proportions sing. The room's width relates to its height as a fifth relates to an octave. The window's height is a golden section of the wall. The column spacing echoes the structural bay. This rightness is not subjective — it is mathematical, and it is felt before it is understood.

We draw on classical principles of harmony and ratio — the golden section, the Fibonacci sequence, modular systems — adapted for contemporary life. These are not decorative overlays; they are the scaffolding of spatial experience. A room proportioned on the golden rectangle feels stable. A facade organized by a modulating grid feels orderly. A stair whose riser and tread follow the Blondel formula feels effortless to climb.

The Golden Section in Practice

The golden ratio (1:1.618...) appears throughout nature — in the spiral of a nautilus, the arrangement of leaves, the proportions of the human body. In architecture, it has been used from the Parthenon to the Villa Rotonda to the UN Secretariat. We use it not as a rigid formula but as a proportional guide.

At Villa Solara, the living room's dimensions follow the golden rectangle. The master bedroom's bay window is a golden section of the facade. The infinity pool's length relates to its width by phi. These relationships are not visible as numbers — they are felt as harmony.

Modular Systems and the Human Scale

Le Corbusier's Modulor system — based on the golden section and the human body — remains relevant. A 2.26m ceiling height. A 1.13m window sill. A 0.56m module for joinery. These dimensions derive from the human figure with raised arm, creating a built environment that fits the body that inhabits it.

We adapt modular thinking to each project. At The Quiet House, a 900mm module governs the tatami grid, the shoji dimensions, the step heights. At Maison de Lumière, a 1.2m structural bay organizes the gallery wing. The module is not a constraint — it is a coordinate system that ensures every decision relates to the whole.

Musical Analogies

Architecture and music share a language: rhythm, interval, harmony, proportion. The Pythagorean discovery that consonant intervals correspond to simple numerical ratios (octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, fourth 4:3) applies equally to spatial intervals. A room 4m by 6m (2:3) feels harmonious. A facade divided into bays of 3m and 5m (3:5, a Fibonacci pair) feels right.

We use these ratios intuitively. The Auditorium at Maison de Lumière has a width-to-height ratio of 3:4 (a fourth). The Gallery Wing's bay spacing is 5m (a Fibonacci number). The Cliff House's cantilever is 15m — a multiple of the 3m structural module. These are not arbitrary choices; they are tuned intervals.

Proportion as Sustainability

A well-proportioned building is a sustainable building. Spaces that feel right are spaces that are loved — and loved buildings are maintained, adapted, preserved. The Palazzo Vecchio has survived seven centuries because its proportions make it eternally usable. The Farnsworth House, for all its rigor, struggles because its proportions prioritize idea over inhabitation.

We design for the long now. The proportional systems we embed are not style — they are structure. They will outlast the finishes, the furnishings, the mechanical systems. They are the DNA of the building, ensuring that even as the building changes use, its spatial logic remains coherent.

Proportion is the silent language of architecture. It speaks not in words but in ratios, not in images but in relationships. To master proportion is to design buildings that feel inevitable — as if they could not be otherwise. In a world of noise and novelty, this quiet rightness is the most radical gesture we can make.
